## 🧭 Overview

By *Hereticus*, [[Gregor Eisenhorn]] is no longer merely hunting radicals and heretics — he is one of the figures other inquisitors fear most. The murder of Midas Betancore and the pursuit of the renegade inquisitor Quixos drag Eisenhorn into a final reckoning that spans old battlefields, political vendettas, and the unresolved consequences of everything he embraced in *[[Xenos]]* and *[[Malleus]]*.

The novel closes the original Eisenhorn trilogy with a story of grief, obsession, and irreversible compromise. Eisenhorn’s warband is shattered, his methods are pushed past any easy defence, and his victory comes entwined with the same corruption he has spent three books resisting.

---

## Part One — A Man Under Judgement
*Hereticus* opens with Eisenhorn already transformed by the first two books. He is feared, monitored, and morally isolated long before the main crisis fully breaks over him. That change in status matters: the trilogy’s last volume is not only about defeating an enemy, but about confronting what kind of inquisitor Eisenhorn has become in the process.

The attack on the people closest to him turns the novel’s conflict personal in a way the previous books only foreshadowed. Loss does not correct Eisenhorn’s trajectory; it hardens it.

---

## Part Two — Obsession and Pursuit
The hunt that follows brings the whole Eisenhorn arc back into focus. Quixos is the immediate target, but the wider web of enemies ensures the book keeps circling back to everything Eisenhorn failed to bury in earlier volumes. [[Pontius Glaw]] remains an important part of that wider corruption, and the [[Pontius Glaw Investigation]] becomes one of the threads through which the trilogy forces Eisenhorn to revisit every earlier bargain.

At the same time, the novel keeps pressure on the question of legitimacy. Eisenhorn may still be fighting genuine threats, but he no longer has unquestioned authority to do so. That tension is one of the book’s great strengths: the reader can understand why he acts while also seeing why others would call him dangerous.

---

## Part Three — Victory Without Innocence
The ending of *Hereticus* matters because it denies the comfort of clean restoration. Eisenhorn can survive, and he can even prevail, but he cannot return to the man he was in *[[Xenos]]*. The methods he has embraced and the losses he has endured have become part of him permanently.

As a trilogy capstone, the novel succeeds by turning inward. The final question is not whether heresy can be beaten, but whether someone who relies on corrupt tools to defeat corruption can still claim moral difference from the things he hunts. Eisenhorn’s answer is effectively yes. The novel’s answer is much more troubled.

## 🧠 Themes
- Obsession overtaking duty
- The impossibility of fighting heresy without contamination
- Grief as a driver of radical action

## 📝 Notes
- [[Alizebeth Bequin]] remains crucial because she measures the human cost of Eisenhorn’s choices.
- [[Pontius Glaw]] functions as both antagonist and embodiment of unfinished corruption.
- The trilogy ends strongly by making victory feel real but spiritually compromised.
- *Hereticus* is the point where Eisenhorn stops being a merely embattled inquisitor and becomes a cautionary one.

## 🔗 Connections
- Concludes the main arc begun in *[[Xenos]]* and intensified in *[[Malleus]]*.
- Brings the [[Pontius Glaw Investigation]] to its resolution.
- Reframes [[Gregor Eisenhorn]] as one of the defining examples of inquisitorial radicalisation in 40K fiction.
